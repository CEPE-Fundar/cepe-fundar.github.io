Odysseus in the Lab

Innovación
What AI Can’t Learn From Us
Autor/a

Eduardo Levy Yeyati

Fecha de publicación

22 de agosto de 2026

A recent piece in Argentina’s La Nación flagged something that sounds paradoxical: while AI threatens white-collar jobs across the board, it has become one of the best employers of philosophers. Anthropic, OpenAI, and DeepMind hire them to write “constitutions,” referee edge cases, and argue about what a model should do when the rules run out. The Economist made the same point in May 2026, reporting that philosophy majors now have meaningfully lower unemployment than computer science majors: roughly 5% versus 7%, based on NACE and New York Fed data.

The obvious question is why a technology built to reason at superhuman speed and scale would need philosophers at all. The less obvious, more interesting question is what exactly it is that philosophers — or more precisely, human practical judgment — bring to the table that the model doesn’t already have.

More precisely, what part of how humans learn to judge can’t be learned by a machine, no matter how much data or compute you throw at it.

Practical wisdom, briefly

Aristotle had a word for the kind of judgment this is about: phronesis, usually translated as “practical wisdom”. Not knowledge of universal rules (that’s closer to what we’d call “deontology”), not cost-benefit calculation (that’s “utilitarianism”). Rather, the capacity to see, in a particular situation, what the right thing to do is – especially when the values at stake pull in different directions. Compassion and justice conflict. Honesty and kindness conflict. Phronesis doesn’t resolve that conflict by rule, but by judgment, the kind formed over time by practice (Aristotle’s praxis) rather than by deduction.

That formation-by-doing is the key here. Phronesis isn’t downloaded, it’s built through repeated decisions, through getting things wrong, through the accumulation of a life.

What, exactly, does an AI system lack that keeps it from building this?

I think the honest answer has two parts. The most obvious one is a hard engineering problem that may eventually soften with better tools. The second one is the philosophical core, and it doesn’t soften at all.

The first obstacle: tacit knowledge

The easy answer, and the one people usually think of first, is that AI hasn’t lived. It hasn’t aged, made a fool of itself, felt shame, watched a decision go wrong over years instead of milliseconds. There’s something to this, but as a full, forward-looking explanation it’s weaker than it sounds.

Michael Polanyi’s old observation is the sharper version of this intuition: “we know more than we can tell” (The Tacit Dimension, University of Chicago Press, p. 4). A lot of expert judgment (a surgeon’s hands, a trader’s read of a room, a manager’s sense that someone is about to quit) is encoded in ways the expert can’t fully articulate, which makes it hard to teach directly and thus hard to codify into a system. Economist David Autor later gave this its working name, “Polanyi’s paradox,” in a 2014 paper on automation and the shape of employment growth.

This is a real obstacle as well as a potential threat for the human worker: as I argued elsewhere, this could lead to an “atrophy trap,” a genuine risk when practice is outsourced to a machine before the human has built the underlying skill.

But this is a problem of information, not of constitution. A mentor transmits judgment to someone younger without that person having lived every case themselves, through story, correction, apprenticeship, watching enough decisions unfold to recognize the pattern before living it firsthand. In principle, a system exposed to a large enough range of cases, outcomes, and consequences (not lived but recorded) may approximate the same pattern-recognition.

It would be an imperfect substitute, possibly a bad one today, but not one ruled out by definition: better architecture, better data, and better simulation could, over time, erode that threshold.

The second obstacle: skin in the game

Here’s the deep one. Aristotle’s account of phronesis is explicitly tied to eudaimonia: one’s own flourishing. Practical deliberation, for Aristotle, is deliberation about what is good for the agent doing the deliberating. The judgment is only phronesis, and not some other, thinner kind of cleverness, because the person exercising it has something of their own riding on getting it right: their character, their future, their own account of what a good life looks like.

No volume of simulated cases gives a system something to lose.

A model can process a million examples of people facing hard trade-offs and still, in its millionth-and-first judgment, risk nothing of its own: no reputation, no identity, no continuity across time that the decision could damage. Simulating risk and bearing risk are not the same operation, and the gap between them doesn’t close with scale.

It’s the kind of wisdom Homer’s Odysseus builds the hard way, correcting course through consequences he has to live with, something a rulebook couldn’t have handed him in advance.

In “A Critique of Utilitarianism,” Bernard Williams gives the sharpest version of the human side of this: utilitarian calculation asks individual agents to treat their own “ground projects” (the commitments around which they have built their lives) as one satisfaction among others, weighable and dispensable if the sum comes out that way. The clearest illustration is a later essay of his, “Persons, Character and Morality,” where a man saves his wife rather than two strangers who are also drowning, simply because she is his wife. Williams’s point is that looking for a further, impartial justification for that choice is already “one thought too many”.

A case that shows rather than tells

With less authority and style, this is at the core of what I elsewhere called the moral distancing of artificial intelligence. Imagine you can save exactly one of two people from certain death: your mother, or a scientist on the verge of a cancer cure. Most people save their mother because they can’t see themselves as the kind of person who would trade her for a stranger, however many lives that stranger might eventually save.

No rule solves this cleanly, and no accumulation of similar cases would have prepared a purely pattern-matching system to solve it “correctly,” because there is no outcome-independent fact about which choice is correct. What there is, instead, is a person for whom one of the two outcomes is unbearable in a way the other isn’t. That unbearability is not itself a data point the system could have learned from watching enough dilemmas unfold. It must be lived, by someone with something to lose in the tradeoff.

That’s the piece the tacit-knowledge story leaves out. You could, in principle, train a system to predict what humans tend to choose in this dilemma, and even to reproduce the reasoning humans give afterward. That’s the first, solvable obstacle.

You can’t train a system for whom the choice constitutes anything, for whom choosing the scientist would mean becoming, and having to live as, the kind of agent who chose the scientist. That’s the second obstacle, and it goes beyond engineering.

None of says whether we should let systems make these calls, but this piece is narrower: it’s about what phronesis needs to be built, not exercised: it needs a kind of exposure no dataset can provide, the exposure of having something of your own on the line when the judgment is made.

The philosophers hired by AI labs right now are very good at articulating rules and weighing consequences. What none of them can hand over, no matter how carefully they write the constitution, is the thing that made their own judgment worth hiring in the first place.

(The philosophers surely know it but keep that for themselves.)
